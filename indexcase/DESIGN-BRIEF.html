Unknown aetiologyDesign brief · draft 2

Index Case

A new disease nobody has seen before appears in a city of a quarter of a million people. You lead the public health response. Work out what it is while it spreads, and decide what to do before you are sure.

Working title. The index case is the first case that brings an outbreak to attention, not necessarily the first person infected. Finding the real first person is one of the game's threads.

01The idea

Every game generates a new pathogen with its own biology, and a city for it to spread through. You don't know how it spreads, how long it hides, whether people pass it on before they feel ill, or who it kills. Neither does anyone else. You find out from the data, and the data arrives late, partial and noisy.

The game runs in three acts. It starts as a detective story at the scale of one hospital ward and, if you are slow or unlucky, becomes a strategy game at the scale of a city. The same deduction runs all the way through: every decision is only as good as your current understanding of the disease.

Act oneDetectA strange pneumonia cluster: four patients at St Anne's, a care home, a wedding. Most people think it's flu. You decide whether it's something new, and you are the only one who can get the lab to look.
Act twoCharacteriseWrite the case definition. Estimate incubation, the route, spread before symptoms, hidden cases and severity by age. Sequence samples and read the family tree. Find where it came from.
Act threeContainTesting, tracing, isolation, masks, closures, what to tell the public. Hospital capacity, trust, the council, the press and rumours. Hold on until treatment or a vaccine arrives.

02The pathogen generator

This is what makes every game different. Each run draws a disease from these hidden traits. The ranges are wide on purpose, and the traits are drawn together so the result is biologically plausible (a disease that kills most of the people it infects rarely spreads silently at the same time).

TraitRangeHow you work it out
Routeairborne · droplet · contact · gut · animalWhich settings produce clusters: a stuffy choir practice (airborne) versus households only (droplet or contact), a shared kitchen (gut), cases with no human contact at all (animal source still spilling over).

Hidden cases0 – 70% never illTest every contact in a household, symptoms or not, and see how many are positive but well.
Spread rateR 1.2 – 5How fast the epidemic curve doubles, and how many people each case infects in traced chains.
Severitydeath 0.1% – 12%Hospital admissions and deaths against confirmed cases, corrected for the cases you never find.
Who it hitselderly · young adults · children · evenAdmissions by age compared with infections by age. The 1918 flu killed young adults; COVID killed the old.
Symptoms4–7 from ~20Frequencies across confirmed cases. Some diseases have a tell (a rash, loss of taste, red eyes) that makes the case definition easy; others look exactly like flu.

03How deduction works

A worked example: reading the genome tree. You have sent eight samples for sequencing. Each branch point is a mutation; cases on the same twig are closely linked.

Two separate branches mean two separate introductions, not one outbreak. The nurse sits between the ward and the care home, so she probably carried it between them, which says something about spread between staff, maybe before symptoms. The delivery driver sits at the root of the wedding branch but was never at the wedding. So check his route: who else did he deliver to? Each of those is a hypothesis you file and test with the next round of tracing and sequencing.

Estimates, not answers. You publish your working estimates as you go ("incubation about 5 days; roughly 30% of cases never have symptoms; airborne indoors"), and the game compares them with the truth only in the debrief. Your estimates drive your policy, and in the game the public and the council react to what you publish. Revising publicly costs some trust; being wrong for too long costs more.

Everything else in the investigation: the case line list; a city map with cases and clusters; the epidemic curve with a "nowcast" band for cases not yet reported; contact-tracing diagrams; household studies; hospital admissions by age; wastewater readings by district, which rise days before cases do; and exposure questionnaires for clusters.

04The city underneath

A generated city of about 250,000 people, simulated as around 8,000 representative residents so it runs smoothly on a phone. Each has an age, a household, a job or school, a neighbourhood, a commute, habits (pub, gym, church, choir, football) and a level of trust in authority. Places have size, crowding and ventilation. The disease spreads through those actual contacts, so the clusters you find are real.

Noise is part of the design: an ordinary flu season running alongside, doctors who don't recognise the new disease, test shortages, people who won't say where they were, and a newspaper that sometimes gets there first.

AreaActionsLimited by

ContainIsolation, quarantine of contacts, targeted closures (a venue, a school), gathering limits, masks indoors, ventilation guidance, travel advice, a city-wide lockdown as a last resort.Trust, money, political will. Each order has a lag before it shows in the curve.
ProtectHospital surge planning, shielding advice for the vulnerable group, care home visiting rules.Beds and staff. An overwhelmed hospital raises the death rate for everyone.
CommunicatePress briefings, published estimates, guidance, answering rumours.Credibility. Saying too little breeds rumours; saying something wrong costs you later.

Pressure

  • Trust and compliance by neighbourhood and age group. They fall when orders feel pointless or unfair, and rise when your estimates prove right.
  • The council and the mayor (fictional) want the city open; the hospital wants it shut; local businesses, schools and the press all push.
  • Rumours spread through the same social network as the disease, and a bad one ("it's in the water", "the test causes it") changes behaviour.
  • The cure clock: once you have characterised the pathogen and published its sequence, a vaccine or treatment arrives some weeks later. The better your characterisation, the sooner it lands.

06Winning

  • Lives: deaths and hospitalisations compared with a "ghost city" in which nobody acted, shown side by side at the end.
  • Understanding: how close your published estimates were to the truth, and how early you had them.
  • Cost: days of closures, money spent, trust left.
  • The origin: finding the real first case and the source.
  • The debrief: the true outbreak replayed on the map, infection by infection, with the real disease card revealed next to your estimates.

A short game can end at act two (you characterised it fast and it never escaped). A long one runs to the vaccine.

07Handling the subject

COVID is recent and painful for many people. The game treats it seriously: the pathogens are fictional, there are no real politicians or institutions to mock, deaths are shown as people with names and families rather than as a score to farm, and there is no "infect the world" mode. It aims for the feel of the film Contagion: procedural, humane and tense, never gleeful.

08Look and feel

A modern incident room: a large city map with cases and districts, printouts, a whiteboard of estimates, the genome tree on a second screen, the phone that rings when the mayor calls. Clean, clinical data design (line lists, curves, trees, dashboards) that makes patterns readable at a glance, with the illustrated style from Covert Action for people, interviews and places. Phone-first: Map, Cases, Lab, Actions and Briefing tabs, with the map and tree pinch-to-zoom.
