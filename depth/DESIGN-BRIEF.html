Design brief · for approval

Depth

A spy ring is talking over the air. You can hear every word and understand none of it. Find them, read them, stop them.

01 · The premise

Autumn 1977. You are a signals analyst at Station Kestrel, a listening post on the Baltic coast, watching the port city of Haldmar. An illegal network is preparing an operation there. Its controller abroad talks to it through a numbers station. Its agents answer with Morse and burst transmitters, and use phone boxes and dead drops for the rest.

You never leave the station. Everything you learn comes through a receiver, a map, a pencil and a pad. You hand targets to Special Branch, who do the arresting. Every raid you call costs a warrant, and a bad one warns the ring.

Taken from CAA new hidden network every game, running to its own calendar. Several routes to the truth, each a skill game. Arrests need proof.
Left behindBreak-ins, gunfights and car chases. Skill games that drop a random fact. An enemy that never notices you.
The ruleNo instrument ever tells you a fact. Each one gives you material, and how well you play decides how good it is. You do the concluding.
02 · A shift

Play is split into shifts: eight in-game hours, about 10 to 15 minutes of real time, so one fits on a train. Each task costs station time, and the network keeps moving whether you are listening or not.

ListenSweep the bands, catch what is on air, copy it down cleanly.
FixAsk the outstations for bearings and plot where the transmitter is.
BreakPut messages on the workbench and look for a way in.
ChartLog who talks to whom and when. The shape of the ring shows up here first.
ActWatch a house, lift a letter box, or raid. Spend warrants carefully.
03 · The instruments

Four skill games, one case file

The receiver

A live waterfall display of the shortwave band, with static, fading and interference. You tune, set the bandwidth, pick the mode, and copy what you hear into the log.

  • Voice numbers from the controller: five-figure groups read by a flat synthetic voice, on a timetable you learn.
  • Morse from agents, each with a recognisable touch on the key. Over time you learn to tell one operator from another by ear.
  • Burst transmissions: a second of noise you only catch if you were already on the frequency.

Your copy quality decides how many groups come through garbled. A garbled group can wreck a decrypt later on.

Copy from a poor night. Two groups are damaged. You can come back for the repeat broadcast, which is exactly what the ring relies on you not doing.

Direction finding

When an agent's set is on air, you ask outstations for bearings. Each one gives a line with an error cone, and the size of the cone depends on how long the signal lasts and how good the station is. Where the cones overlap on the city map is where the set probably is.

  • Short transmissions give wide cones, so you plot across several nights and narrow it down.
  • Some sets move in vans. A fix that lands somewhere different every night is itself a clue.
  • At the end you can send a direction-finding van into the streets. That is a hot-and-cold search with a signal meter, and it is the only real-time part of the game.

What you get is a search area measured in city blocks, not an address. The tighter you make it, the cheaper the raid.

Three bearings on a 40-second Morse transmission. The overlap is four blocks by the river.

The workbench

Real cryptanalysis, made playable. A one-time pad used properly cannot be broken, so you look for where people got lazy. Every network is generated with human weaknesses in it.

  • Depth: two messages sent with the same key page. Line them up, guess a likely word in one (a crib), and see whether the other message turns into sense. That is what the game is named after.
  • Hand ciphers used by low-level couriers, such as a straddling checkerboard with a transposition. They are weaker, so you attack them with frequency counts and patterns.
  • Operator habits: the same opening group every Tuesday, a sign-off that never changes, the date written into the indicator.

The result is a partial decrypt with holes in it. The holes are real; you fill them from context or leave them open.

Two messages sent on the same key page. Guess TUESDAY at position 3 in one and the other reads HARBOUR. (Shown one letter per digit to keep it readable.) Nobody hands you that; it is a guess you placed yourself.

Traffic analysis

You often can't read a message, but you can always see that it was sent. Your log builds a picture of the ring: callsigns, times, message lengths, who replies and after how long. From that you draw a diagram showing who gives orders, who passes them on, and who acts.

  • A spike of long messages before a quiet spell means something is about to happen.
  • A new callsign appearing means the ring has recruited someone, or is testing you.
  • When you raid someone, their contacts go quiet. That confirms the links, and it also tells the ring you are on to them.

You draw the network diagram yourself, and the game marks each link you draw by how much evidence backs it.

Links in solid ink are backed by evidence. The dashed one is your hunch and stays marked as a hunch until you prove it.
04 · The other side

They listen too

Counter-measures

The ring has a security officer, and he reacts to what you do. After a raid they change key pages, frequencies and timetables. After a near miss they send decoy traffic that goes nowhere. A careless operator gets replaced by a better one. The ring learns from you just as you learn from them.

The operation

Every network is generated around a plot with a date: sabotage during a NATO exercise, getting a defector out, a theft from the naval yard. Traffic rises towards that date. Read it in time and you can be waiting at the right quay.

Warrants and trust

Special Branch gives you a few warrants a week. Watching a house is cheap, lifting a dead drop costs more, and a raid costs most. A wrong raid costs warrants, the ring's trust in its own security (which makes them tighten up), and your superintendent's patience.

A mole at Kestrel?

On harder grades the ring may have someone inside your station. Tip-offs arrive at bad moments. It is the double agent from Covert Action, turned into something you can catch with a planted false schedule.

06 · Look, sound, phone

Look. Painted instrument panels and paper: a green waterfall display, grey log sheets, a pencil for red rings and notes, a street map with a tracing-paper overlay. Close to Cutout's desk, but at night, with the equipment switched on.

Sound matters most. All of it is synthesised: static that breathes, Morse with each operator's own rhythm, the numbers voice, the interval tune that plays before each broadcast. It needs headphones, and you can hear a burst transmission a moment before the display shows it.

Phone first. Tuning is a big drag dial and copying is a number pad, so there is almost no typing. The workbench is drag and drop. Everything is turn-based except the van search. The game autosaves at the end of every shift.

07 · How it gets built
M1The ring and the air. Generator for a network, its plot, schedules and operator habits; simulation of the traffic; the receiver with synthesised audio; the log sheet. At this point you can play a shift where you only listen.
M2Breaking and fixing. The workbench with depth, cribs and checkerboards; direction finding on the city map; a solver that checks every generated ring can be broken with the material available.
M3Acting and reacting. Traffic diagram, warrants and raids, the security officer's counter-measures, the plot date, the end-of-case report.
M4A career. A tutorial ring, difficulty grades, the mole, the van search, station improvements between cases (a new outstation, a teleprinter, time on the big computer), and hints in the same style as Cutout's night analyst.

It will be a single HTML file like the others, and I'll publish playable builds as each milestone lands.

08 · Decisions for you
  1. Setting. Baltic, 1977, as written? Or a present-day version, which would change the tools to phones, metadata, encrypted apps and satellites?
  2. How real the codebreaking should be. I'd make the techniques real but give plenty of help: frequency counts, suggested crib positions, and the checkerboard drawn out for you. Or do you want it harder and less assisted?
  3. The van search. Keep it as the one real-time moment, or keep the whole game at a desk?
  4. Length. I'd aim for a case to take 4 to 6 shifts, about an hour. Longer and more like a campaign, or shorter?
