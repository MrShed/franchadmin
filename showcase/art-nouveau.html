The Whiplash Linea salon of Art Nouveau

For twenty luminous years, European designers tried to make everything — a metro entrance, a hair comb, a chocolate advertisement — grow like a plant. The poster on this page is drawn in their manner: choose a season, and watch the ornament return.

A new art, for a new century

By the 1880s Europe was drowning in revival styles — neo-Gothic banks, neo-Renaissance railway stations, reproduction Louis XV chairs. A generation of artists, many inspired by William Morris and the English Arts and Crafts movement, and by the flood of Japanese prints arriving in Paris, wanted something that belonged to their own time.

They found it in nature: not copied, but abstracted into rhythm. Stems became structural lines, petals became panels, and a single S-shaped curve — the coup de fouet, or whiplash — became the signature of an era.

The name came from a shop. In December 1895 the dealer Siegfried Bing opened La Maison de l'Art Nouveau in Paris, selling Tiffany glass, Japanese objects and new furniture. The style peaked at the 1900 Exposition Universelle, and was already fading by the First World War, overtaken by the straight lines of Art Deco and modernism.

Figures of the salon

  • Alphonse Mucha1860–1939

    A struggling Czech illustrator until, over Christmas 1894, he designed Gismonda for Sarah Bernhardt. Paris woke to find it on every wall. His women, halos and mosaic borders became the "Mucha style".

  • Hector Guimard1867–1942

    Architect of the Paris Métro entrances (from 1900): green cast-iron stems, amber lamps like flower buds.

  • Victor Horta1861–1947

    Built the first true Art Nouveau houses in Brussels, where structure itself is ornament.

  • Émile Gallé1846–1904

    Glassmaker and botanist of the Nancy school; engraved verses and dragonflies into cameo glass.

  • René Lalique1860–1945

    Jewels of enamel, horn and opal shaped as dragonfly-women and thistles.

  • Louis Comfort Tiffany1848–1933

    New York's master of favrile glass and leaded lamps, sold in Paris by Bing.

  • Hermann Obrist1862–1927

    His embroidered cyclamen of 1895 was nicknamed Peitschenhieb — "whiplash" — and gave the line its name.

  • Aubrey Beardsley1872–1898

    Black-and-white drawings of wicked elegance for Salome and The Yellow Book.

Principles of the style

The whiplash curve. A line that accelerates, snaps and relaxes, like a stem in wind — never a compass arc.
Nature, abstracted. Irises, lilies, poppies, dragonflies, peacocks: studied from life, then stylised into pattern.
The total work of art. The same hand designs the building, the door handle, the menu and the dinner plates.
No borrowed history. No columns from Greece or arches from Gothic cathedrals. Invent the ornament fresh.
New materials made beautiful. Iron, glass and lithographic ink — industrial media given organic grace.
Art in the street. Posters, shop fronts, Métro stations: beauty for the public, not only the salon.

About this poster

It borrows Mucha's grammar: a horseshoe arch, a woman in profile against a golden halo, a mosaic ring of circles, hair that behaves like vegetation, and lettering set in a dark panel at the foot. Mucha's own Seasons series of 1896 inspired the four variations — each changes the palette, the flowers in the ring and the colour of the hair.

The frame is drawn with SVG strokes that animate from nothing, the way a lithographer's key-line would come first. The hair is regenerated every frame, swaying gently unless your device asks for reduced motion.
