The Fossil Cabineta catalogue of eight drawers

In 1887 the Reverend Absalom Pellow left the museum his collector's cabinet: forty-eight oak drawers, lined in green baize, each with a brass label-holder and a pencilled accession number. Most of the drawers now live in the stores. Eight of them are open to you here.

On keeping stones that were animals

A fossil is not a bone. It is a replacement: over thousands or millions of years, mineral-laden water seeps through buried remains and deposits crystals, grain by grain, in the spaces where the living tissue used to be. What you are looking at in these drawers is stone that has taken the shape of something else, preserved so faithfully that the growth lines on a trilobite's eye can still be counted.

Pellow's generation catalogued their finds on index cards, typed or written in iron-gall ink, and we have kept that tradition. Each specimen carries an accession number — the year it entered the collection and its sequence that year — so that any drawer, from any decade, can be traced back to the day someone first wrote it into the register. Several of our most famous pieces are casts, and we say so on the card: the original Archaeopteryx and the Tyrannosaurus are far too precious to live in a provincial cabinet, but the plaster remembers them well.

The strip of colour above is the Phanerozoic, the eon of visible life, drawn to scale. Notice how much of it has already passed before the first dinosaur appears, and how thin the slice is in which anything resembling us has walked about. The drawers are small. The time they hold is not.
