Est. MCMXXIV · beneath the florist · Wabash Avenue

The Velvet

Cocktails, of which we know nothing, officer.

On the sixteenth of January 1920, the Eighteenth Amendment made it illegal to manufacture, sell or transport intoxicating liquor anywhere in the United States. Within a year, America had more places to get a drink than it had before. They hid behind barbershops, beneath laundries, at the back of soda fountains and, in our case, under a florist whose front window was always, suspiciously, full of lilies.

The name speakeasy is older than the law, borrowed from Irish and British slang for an unlicensed grog shop — a place where you spoke softly so as not to draw the constable. The rule was the same on Wabash Avenue: a password at the door, a quiet voice at the bar, and not a word about any of it upstairs.

Bootleg liquor was frequently terrible, and much of the invention of the era came from bartenders trying to hide it. Honey, citrus, grenadine and sparkling wine could make bathtub gin taste like a rumour of something better. Many of the best American bartenders simply left — to London, Paris and Havana — and the drinks they wrote down there came home when Repeal did, on the fifth of December 1933.

The list below is our small tribute to those thirteen dry years. Every drink is one that was poured, or first set down in print, in the decade either side of Prohibition. Specs are the house's own; the strength is calculated from the pour and a fair allowance for the ice.

Rules

  1. If a gentleman in a bowler hat asks for a sarsaparilla, pour him one. Then pour him another. Then hum loudly.
  2. When the bell above the bar rings twice, every glass goes into the sink and every sink goes into the wall. Practise this.
  3. We do not ask your name and you do not ask ours. The pianist's name is Leonard. Everybody knows that.
  4. Voices low. The florist is a light sleeper and an excellent witness.
  5. Nobody leaves by the front. The alley door opens onto the coal chute. Mind the step, and your hat.
