The Cloud Genera

Every cloud you will ever see belongs to one of ten genera. The scheme was sketched by a Quaker pharmacist, Luke Howard, in a lecture of 1802, when he gave Latin names to things everyone had always seen and nobody had thought to sort: cirrus, a curl of hair; cumulus, a heap; stratus, a layer; nimbus, a rain cloud. The World Meteorological Organization still builds its International Cloud Atlas on his bones.

The genera are grouped by height into three étages. We begin at the top, where the air is forty below and the clouds are made of ice, and descend. The sky to the left follows you down.

High étage5 – 13 km · ice crystals
Ci

Cirrus

cirrus — a lock of hair, a tendril
Base
6 – 12 km
Made of
Ice crystals
Precip.
Never reaches ground

Detached white filaments, often ending in hooks or tufts, drawn across a deep blue sky. What you are seeing is ice crystals falling out of a cloud head and being stretched by winds that change speed with height; the streaks are fall-streaks, a kind of snow that evaporates long before it gets anywhere.

Sailors called them mares' tails, and an old rhyme warns that "mares' tails and mackerel scales make tall ships take in their sails". Thickening cirrus spreading from the west is often the first herald of a warm front a day away.

LOOK FOR hooks (uncinus), dense patches (spissatus), and sunsets: cirrus lights up long after lower clouds have gone grey.

Cc

Cirrocumulus

cirrus + cumulus — a heap of curls
Base
6 – 12 km
Made of
Ice, some supercooled water
Element size
< 1° wide

A thin sheet of tiny white grains or ripples, often arranged in rows, with no shading at all. Hold your arm out: each element should be smaller than your little fingernail. Anything bigger is probably altocumulus.

This is the true mackerel sky, patterned like the scales on a fish's back. It is one of the less common genera and rarely lasts long — the small droplets that help form it freeze quickly, turning the ripples to cirrus or cirrostratus within minutes.

LOOK FOR fine ripples like sand on a beach (undulatus), and the absence of grey undersides.

Cs

Cirrostratus

cirrus + stratus — a spread of curls
Base
6 – 12 km
Made of
Ice crystals
Tell-tale
22° halo

A transparent, whitish veil that may cover the whole sky so thinly you barely notice it — until you see the halo. Hexagonal ice crystals refract sunlight at 22°, painting a ring around the sun or moon about the width of an outstretched hand.

The sun remains sharp-edged and still casts shadows, which is how you tell cirrostratus from the thicker altostratus below. A veil that thickens through the day, turning the halo faint, is a reliable sign that rain is coming within twelve to twenty-four hours.

LOOK FOR a halo, sun dogs (bright spots either side of the sun), and a milky sky that still allows shadows.

Middle étage2 – 7 km · water & ice
Ac

Altocumulus

altus + cumulus — a high heap
Base
2 – 7 km
Made of
Water droplets
Element size
1 – 5°

Rounded white or grey masses in sheets, rows or waves, with some shading on their undersides. Each element is about the width of one to three fingers held at arm's length. It is the most varied of all the genera and turns up in every season and nearly every weather.

When the sun or moon shines through thin altocumulus, you may see a corona — coloured rings close around the disc, caused by diffraction on water droplets rather than refraction through ice. On humid summer mornings, turreted altocumulus castellanus is a classic warning of afternoon thunder.

LOOK FOR shading, corona, lens shapes over hills (lenticularis), and small towers (castellanus).

As

Altostratus

altus + stratus — a high layer
Base
2 – 7 km
Made of
Water & ice
Tell-tale
Ground-glass sun

A grey or bluish sheet, fibrous or uniform, covering all or much of the sky. The sun shows through it only dimly, as if through frosted glass, and casts no shadow. There is no halo. It is the dullest of clouds and the most honest: altostratus usually means the front is nearly here.

It rarely produces much itself, though light rain or snow may fall from it. Watch the sun fade over an hour; when it vanishes entirely and the base lowers, the altostratus is becoming nimbostratus.

LOOK FOR a watery sun without shadows and a sky the colour of a pewter plate.

Ns

Nimbostratus

nimbus + stratus — a raining layer
Base
0.5 – 3 km
Depth
Up to 5 km+
Precip.
Steady rain or snow

A dark grey, thick, featureless layer, the true rain cloud of a British winter. It blots out the sun completely and has a diffuse, wet-looking base, often hidden by ragged scud racing beneath it. The rain it produces is continuous rather than showery, and it can go on for hours.

Classed with the middle étage by tradition, it often extends from near the ground into the high levels. You are unlikely to admire it, but it deserves respect: most of the water in most rivers began here.

LOOK FOR total grey, steady precipitation, no visible edges, and pannus — dark shreds below.

Low étage0 – 2 km · water droplets
Sc

Stratocumulus

stratus + cumulus — a layered heap
Base
0.5 – 2 km
Made of
Water droplets
Element size
> 5°

Grey or whitish patches, rolls or lumps, with dark parts, almost always with shading — each element bigger than your fist at arm's length. It is the most common cloud on Earth, covering vast areas of the oceans and much of a typical British day.

Stratocumulus often forms when cumulus spreads out under a temperature inversion, or when a stratus layer breaks up. It may give a little drizzle but seldom anything more. Sunbeams slanting through the gaps — crepuscular rays — are its great consolation.

LOOK FOR big soft rolls, blue gaps, and sun rays fanning between them.

St

Stratus

stratus — spread out, laid flat
Base
0 – 0.5 km
Made of
Water droplets
Precip.
Drizzle, snow grains

A low, grey, uniform layer with a base so near the ground it often hides the tops of hills and tall buildings. Fog that lifts becomes stratus; stratus that sinks to the ground becomes fog. Through thin stratus the outline of the sun is clearly visible.

It forms when moist air cools gently — over cold seas, after rain, on still autumn nights. It is the sea fret of the east coast, the haar of Scotland, the grey lid of a November morning that burns off by eleven, or doesn't.

LOOK FOR hilltops in cloud, drizzle, and ragged fractus shreds.

Cu

Cumulus

cumulus — a heap, a pile
Base
0.6 – 2 km
Made of
Water droplets
Shape
Flat base, domed top

Detached, dense clouds with sharp outlines, flat bases and cauliflower tops that are brilliant white in sunlight. They are the clouds children draw. Each marks the top of a thermal — a column of warm air rising from sun-heated ground — and all their bases sit at the same height, where the rising air cools to its dew point.

Small, flat humilis means settled weather. When cumulus grows taller than it is wide — congestus — by early afternoon, watch it: it is on its way to becoming the next genus.

LOOK FOR a level line of bases, sharp crisp tops, and gliders circling beneath them.

Cb

Cumulonimbus

cumulus + nimbus — a raining heap
Base
0.5 – 2 km
Top
Up to 12 – 16 km
Brings
Thunder, hail, squalls

The king of clouds: a heavy, dense tower of enormous vertical extent, rising from a dark ragged base to a summit that spreads against the tropopause into a smooth, fibrous anvil of ice. A single mature cell may hold hundreds of thousands of tonnes of water.

It is the only genus that produces thunder and lightning, and often hail, gusting squalls and torrential showers. Its whole life, from the first bubbling cumulus to the collapse of its anvil, may last less than an hour. If you can hear the thunder, you are close enough to be struck.

LOOK FOR an anvil (incus), pouches beneath it (mamma), a rain shaft, and the smell of wet dust before the storm.
