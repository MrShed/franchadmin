A guide for the first-time guest

In praise of a bowl of tea

Chanoyu — literally hot water for tea — is the Japanese art of preparing and serving powdered green tea to guests. It asks for about four hours, a small room, some charcoal, and your whole attention. What it gives back is harder to describe.

This is an outline of an ordinary gathering, of the tools on the mat, and of the one gesture everyone remembers: the whisking.

和敬清寂

Four words over the door

In the sixteenth century the tea master Sen no Rikyū stripped the ceremony of the gold and imported porcelain his patrons loved, and left something austere: a thatched hut, a rough local bowl, a single flower. He is credited with the four principles that still govern it. They are less rules than a description of how the room should feel when everything is going well.

和
Wa · harmonyBetween host and guest, between the utensils and the season, between the room and the garden outside it.
敬
Kei · respectFor each person and object, shown in bows and in the careful, unhurried handling of every tool.
清
Sei · purityThe ritual cleaning of the utensils in front of the guests, which is also a clearing of the mind.
寂
Jaku · tranquillityThe stillness that arrives, if you are lucky, once the other three are in place.
点前

The gathering, in order

A full formal chaji includes a meal and two kinds of tea. This is the shape of a simpler gathering for thin tea, usucha, as a guest will experience it. Every school — Urasenke, Omotesenke, Mushakōjisenke — varies the details. The order holds.

  1. 一

    Roji · the dewy pathWalk through the garden and wash

    Guests gather in a waiting arbour, then follow a path of stepping stones laid deliberately uneven so you must watch your feet and leave the city behind. At a low stone basin, the tsukubai, you crouch to rinse your hands and mouth with a bamboo ladle.

    The crouch is the point. Everyone, whatever their rank, bends to the water.

    蹲踞The basin is set low on purpose: tsukubai means “to crouch”.
  2. 二

    Nijiriguchi · the crawl-in doorEnter on your knees

    The guests' entrance to a traditional tea room is a square opening barely seventy centimetres high. You kneel, set your fan in front of you, and shuffle through. Samurai were obliged to leave their swords on a rack outside, since there was no way to bring them through.

    Inside, the room may be as small as four and a half tatami mats — about three metres square.

    躙口Rikyū is said to have taken the idea from the tiny doors of fishing boats.
  3. 三

    Tokonoma · the alcoveBow to the scroll and the flower

    Before you sit, go to the alcove and bow. The hanging scroll, chosen by the host for this day alone, sets the theme of the gathering — often a single line of Zen calligraphy. Beneath it stands a flower arranged in the chabana style: one or two stems, as they would grow in a field.

    床の間It is polite to ask about the scroll later. The host will be glad you did.
  4. 四

    Omogashi · the sweetEat before you drink

    A small confection of sweet bean paste, shaped and coloured for the season — a maple leaf in autumn, a plum blossom in late winter — is passed on a lacquer tray. You lift it onto a folded sheet of kaishi paper and eat it in a few bites with a pick.

    The sweetness prepares the tongue for the bitterness of the tea to come.

    主菓子Finish the sweet completely before the tea is served.
  5. 五

    Hakobidashi · carrying inThe host brings the tools

    The host enters through the service door carrying the tea bowl, with the whisk, cloth and scoop arranged inside it, and the tea caddy. Then the waste-water bowl, with the ladle resting on top. Each is set on the mat in a fixed position, its distance measured in the woven rows of the tatami.

    運び出しNothing in the room is placed by chance.
  6. 六

    Fukusa sabaki · the silk clothPurify the caddy and the scoop

    The host folds a square silk cloth, the fukusa, in a sequence so precise that students practise it for months. With it, the lid of the tea caddy is wiped in the shape of the character こ, then the tea scoop from end to end. The utensils are already clean. The wiping is for everyone watching.

    袱紗Men usually carry a purple fukusa, women red or orange.
  7. 七

    Chasen tōshi · inspecting the whiskWarm the bowl, wake the whisk

    Hot water is ladled from the iron kettle into the bowl. The host rests the whisk in it, lifts and turns it, and looks carefully at the tines — a fine bamboo whisk breaks easily, and a broken tine would spoil the foam. The water is poured away and the bowl wiped with a linen cloth.

    茶筅通しThe soaking also softens the bamboo so it bends rather than snaps.
  8. 八

    Ocha o tateru · making teaTwo scoops, one ladle, the whisk

    Two scoops of matcha from the chashaku, a tap on the rim to loosen the last of the powder. A ladle of water just off the boil — about seventy millilitres. Then the whisking: fast, from the wrist, in a zigzag, until a fine pale foam covers the surface, and a final slow circle to lift the whisk out from the centre.

    点てるTry it yourself, below.
  9. 九

    Nomikata · drinkingTurn the bowl, and drink

    The bowl is set before you with its front — its most beautiful side — facing you. Bow to the next guest: “Osaki ni” — excuse me for going before you. Lift the bowl, thank the host silently, turn it clockwise twice so you do not drink from its face, and finish the tea in three and a half sips. The last is a small, audible slurp. It is a compliment.

    お先にWipe the rim with your fingers, then your fingers on the kaishi.
  10. 十

    Haiken · appreciationAdmire the tools, then leave

    After the host cleans the utensils, the principal guest may ask to look more closely at the caddy and scoop. They are passed, examined low to the mat with great care, and returned, with questions: who made the scoop, what poetic name has it been given. Then bows, and the guests crawl out the way they came.

    The host watches them go down the path and does not close the door until they are out of sight.

    拝見Every scoop has a name, often taken from a poem.
点てる

Whisk a bowl of usucha

Drag across the bowl — quickly, back and forth, in a zigzag from the wrist rather than circles from the elbow. The dark tea will lighten as a fine froth rises. Too slow, and the bubbles stay large and coarse; the aim is a surface like fresh moss after rain.

Eight tools on the mat

A tea room holds very little, and everything it holds is chosen. These are the essential utensils, dōgu, of a thin-tea gathering.

一期一会

Ichigo ichie — one time, one meeting. This gathering, with these people, in this weather, will never happen again. Pour the water as if you knew that. A phrase attributed to Ii Naosuke, who wrote it into his own tea teachings in the 1850s.
