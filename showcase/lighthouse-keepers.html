The Last Lighthouse Keepers

For three centuries, someone climbed the stairs at dusk. Then, one November afternoon in Kent, nobody needed to. On the quiet end of a very old job.

Words by Morwenna AchterbergNineteen minutesIssue 41
Read by the light

On the twenty-sixth of November 1998, the keepers of North Foreland lighthouse lowered their flag, handed over the station, and went home. The light did not go out. That was rather the point. It went on flashing five times every twenty seconds over the approaches to the Thames, exactly as it had the night before, except that now the hands that kept it were in an office in Harwich, reading a screen. North Foreland was the last lighthouse in the care of Trinity House to be automated, and with its handover a job that had existed on English coasts since the seventeenth century quietly ceased to exist.

The ServiceTrinity House, chartered by Henry VIII in 1514, is the lighthouse authority for England, Wales and the Channel Islands. Scotland and the Isle of Man are served by the Northern Lighthouse Board; the whole of Ireland by the Commissioners of Irish Lights.

There was a small ceremony. There were speeches, a plaque, a photograph of men in navy jerseys standing in a row against white paint. It is easy, looking at such pictures, to reach for the word romance, and the keepers themselves were usually the first to wince at it. The work was not romantic. It was clerical, mechanical, domestic, and above all repetitive: a life organised around a lamp that could never be allowed to fail, in a building that was frequently trying to be washed off a rock.

And yet something was lost that November, and it was not only employment. It was a particular kind of attention — the attention of a person who looks at the same stretch of sea every night for years and knows, without instruments, when something on it is wrong.

I. The WatchFour hours on, and the lamp never out

A rock station — a lighthouse on an offshore reef, with no land to speak of — was typically manned by three keepers at a time, drawn from a complement of four so that one was always ashore. In the later decades of the service a keeper might spend two months on the rock and one month off, though the relief boat kept to no timetable but the weather's. Reliefs could be delayed for weeks. Keepers learned to ration tobacco and patience in roughly equal measure.

The day was built from watches. Through the night, one man was always awake in the lantern or the service room below it, and the log recorded his hours. Before electrification the light came from paraffin burners whose wicks had to be trimmed, which is why American keepers were called wickies; later, from pressurised vapour burners that hissed like a kettle and could flare dangerously if neglected. The great lenses, some weighing several tonnes, floated on baths of mercury so a clockwork mechanism could turn them, and the clockwork had to be wound by hand, a heavy weight hauled up the tower every half hour or so, like a grandfather clock the size of a house.

In the morning came the cleaning. Brass was polished and glass was polished, and the lens was polished so often that keepers wore linen aprons to avoid scratching it with their buttons. Curtains were drawn round the lantern in daylight, not for privacy but because a lens that can throw a flame twenty miles to sea will just as happily focus the midday sun onto its own burner and start a fire.

Between these duties was an astonishing amount of time and an astonishingly small place in which to spend it. Keepers built ships in bottles, made rugs, carved, painted, studied for exams by correspondence, kept weather records for the Met Office, and cooked. The cooking, by most accounts, was taken seriously. On a tower where the kitchen was a curved room eleven feet across, a man who could make a decent suet pudding was a man whose company you could bear for eight weeks.

II. The RockWhat the sea does to a building

People who have never stood in a rock lighthouse in a gale tend to imagine it as a sturdy thing, and it is — but sturdy the way a tuning fork is sturdy. Keepers describe the whole tower shuddering when a big sea struck; cups walking across tables; spray, in the worst weather, lashing the lantern glass a hundred feet above the rock. The first Eddystone light, a fanciful timber tower built by Henry Winstanley off Plymouth, was swept away in the Great Storm of 1703 with its builder inside it. Its successor, Rudyerd's tower, burned in 1755; its eldest keeper, Henry Hall, was ninety-four, and he claimed that molten lead from the roof had run into his open mouth as he looked up at the fire. The doctors did not believe him. He died twelve days later, and when they opened him they found a flat oval of lead in his stomach.

That is the grim end of lighthouse history, and there is a lot of it. In December 1900 the relief boat reached the Flannan Isles, west of Lewis, and found the station empty. The three keepers — James Ducat, Thomas Marshall and Donald MacArthur — were never found. The clocks had stopped; the lamp was trimmed and ready; one set of oilskins still hung by the door. The most likely explanation is the most ordinary one, a huge sea on the west landing, but the empty table and the stopped clocks have powered poems, films and a century of speculation.

The heroic end is no less real. In September 1838 the paddle steamer Forfarshire struck the Big Harcar rock off the Farne Islands. From the Longstone light, the keeper William Darling and his twenty-two-year-old daughter Grace rowed a coble through heavy seas to reach the survivors, making two trips to bring nine people back. Grace Darling became, briefly and much against her own inclinations, the most famous woman in Britain. She died of tuberculosis four years later.

    III. The SwitchHow a job is automated out of existence

    Automation did not arrive all at once. Unmanned lights with acetylene burners and sun-valves — which shut the gas off at dawn — were in use from the early twentieth century on minor stations. What changed, in the 1970s and 80s, was reliability: electric lamp-changers that could rotate a fresh bulb into place when one failed, standby generators that started themselves, and telemetry that reported faults down a telephone line or radio link. Once a light could tell a distant office that it was unwell, the case for three men living inside it grew thin, and then it was gone.

    The programme was steady and, by the standards of such things, humane. Keepers were redeployed, retrained, retired. Some became attendants for the stations they had kept, visiting to check on the machinery. Others went ashore for good and discovered what it was to sleep through a whole night. In Ireland the Commissioners of Irish Lights completed their automation in March 1997 at the Baily, on Howth Head above Dublin Bay; in Scotland the Northern Lighthouse Board's last keepers left Fair Isle South in March 1998. North Foreland, that autumn, closed the book.

    HelicoptersMany rock stations acquired helipads on top of their lanterns in the 1970s and 80s. Reliefs that once waited weeks on the weather could be done in an afternoon — one of the quieter steps toward automation.

    It would be wrong to say nothing replaced the keepers. Satellite navigation replaced much of what the lights themselves were for, and a modern ship's officer may cross the Channel without ever consciously taking a bearing on one. The lights remain because systems fail and because the sea does not care about systems. Several have been reduced in range, some discontinued; many still burn, now often with LEDs, now often from solar panels bolted to galleries where men once leaned and smoked and watched the weather come in.

    IV. The AttentionWhat a lighthouse keeper actually kept

    Ask former keepers what the job was, and they rarely begin with the lamp. They talk about the log, about the weather observations sent ashore every three hours, about the fog signal — hours or days of it, the building booming like a ship's hull, sleep impossible. They talk about the boats they watched. Keepers were an unofficial coastguard, often the first to see a vessel in trouble, a flare, a capsized dinghy, a fishing boat that should have been home by now.

    That watching is the thing no telemetry reproduces. A lamp-changer knows if a filament has broken. It does not know that the crabber which always passes at four has not passed. The keepers knew — not because anyone had trained them to, but because nobody can look at the same stretch of water every night for a decade without learning its habits.

    Most of the stations are still there. Some are holiday cottages; you can sleep in rooms where keepers slept, and hear on a bad night what they heard. Some are museums, where the old clockwork sits polished behind rope. The rock towers remain as they always were, unvisited and blinking. They do not need anyone now. That was always the ambition — a light that simply works — and it has been achieved. It is only strange, on a clear night on a headland, to watch that familiar beam come round and know that there is no one up there, and has not been for years, and that there was once a person whose job was simply to make sure you could see it.
