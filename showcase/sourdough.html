Coldhouse Bakery No. 07 · the everyday loaf

CountryLoaf.

This is the bread we bake every morning at Coldhouse, and the one we teach first. It is not the most open, the most sour or the most photogenic loaf in the world. It is simply the most dependable: a mild, wheaty sourdough with ten percent whole wheat, a crackling crust, and a crumb that stays soft for three days on a board under a tea towel.

Everything below is written in baker's percentages, which means every ingredient is expressed as a proportion of the total flour. Once you think this way, a recipe stops being a list of grams and becomes a shape — one you can scale to a single loaf or a rack of twelve without a calculator. Though, since you're here, there's one of those too.

02 / SCHEDULE

Two days, forty minutes of work.

Sourdough is mostly waiting. Set the time you'll mix and the temperature of your dough, and the schedule rewrites itself. Warm doughs ferment fast; every degree cooler adds roughly a seventh to the bulk.

bulk ≈ 5 h at 24°C
    03 / NOTES

    What the dough is telling you.

    Clocks are a guide, not a law. These are the signs we actually look for at the bench.

    A ripe levainhas roughly doubled, smells of yoghurt and green apple rather than nail varnish, and a spoonful floats in water. If it has collapsed and smells sharp, it's past its peak: use a little less, or feed again and wait.

    The end of bulkisn't a time, it's a look. The dough should have grown by about half, feel airy and jiggly when you shake the tub, show a few bubbles at the edges, and dome slightly where it meets the container.

    Shaping tensioncomes from dragging the dough towards you on an unfloured patch of bench. The skin should tighten like a drum; if it tears, you've gone too far — let it rest five minutes and try again.

    The cold retardin the fridge slows yeast more than it slows bacteria, which deepens flavour and makes the loaf far easier to score. Twelve to sixteen hours is our sweet spot; up to thirty-six will still bake well.

    Scoringshould be one confident cut at a shallow angle, about 5 mm deep, just off the centre line. The flap that lifts in the oven is the "ear"; it's lovely but it isn't the point.

    Coolingis the step everyone skips and shouldn't. The crumb is still setting for the first hour out of the oven. Cut it hot and it will be gummy; wait, and it will be the bread in the picture.

    Higher hydrationmakes a more open, glossier crumb, but it asks more of your shaping. If you're new to this, stay at 70–72% for a few bakes before pushing past 78%.

    Whole wheatdrinks more water and ferments faster. Above 30%, add two or three points of hydration and keep an eye on the bulk — it may finish an hour early.
