Synopsis of Scenery & Incidents

Act I.“The Last Lamp in Pudding Lane”

  1. Tableau 1.A lamplighter’s garret overlooking the river. Evening. — Jacob’s pride. Nell’s promise.
  2. Tableau 2.The Embankment, the lamps being lit. — A stranger in a silk hat. “Your father owes me, girl.”
  3. Tableau 3.The Harrow garret by night. — The forged note! The constable at the door! Jacob taken!

Act II.“Above & Below”

  1. Tableau 4.The ballroom at Sallow House, Belgravia. — Nell in borrowed silk. The Captain’s wager.
  2. Tableau 5.The kitchens of Sallow House. — Sparrow overhears. The locket and the letter.
  3. Tableau 6.Wapping Old Stairs, at midnight. — Tom Whitlock rows for his life.
The Great River Sensation!A real skiff upon 4,000 gallons of real Thames water, in a fog of Mr. Grell’s own devising

Act III.“The Lamp Relit”

  1. Tableau 7.Newgate. The condemned cell. — Father and daughter.
  2. Tableau 8.The fog, Blackfriars Bridge. — Every lamp on the river goes out at once. Unmasked by gaslight!
  3. Tableau 9.Pudding Lane, at dawn. — The last lamp is lit. Happiness at last.

Notes for the Programme

Nobody in 1887 would have called this play subtle, and nobody went to the Gaslight hoping it would be. Victorian melodrama was the popular entertainment of its century: a theatre of absolute virtue and perfect villainy, of stolen wills, false accusations and last-minute rescues, played to houses of two and three thousand people who hissed, cheered, and occasionally threw things.

It was also a theatre of machines. Managers competed on sensation scenes — spectacular climaxes staged with every technology the age could offer. Audiences came to see real locomotives, real horses on treadmills, real water in tanks beneath the stage. Our river sensation in Act Two borrows from that tradition, as does the fog, which in the original would have been steam piped up through the traps and lit green by limelight.

Mr. Wexford-Brine is our invention, but his plots are faithful to the genre: an honest working father, a daughter sent among the rich, a villain with a moustache and a mortgage. The lamplighters are real enough. London kept thousands of them, walking the streets at dusk with ladders and long poles to light the gas lamps, and again at dawn to put them out.

Gas was also what made the theatre itself possible. Gaslit stages could be dimmed and brightened from a central “gas table”, which meant for the first time that the audience could sit in the dark and look at a lit picture. It was dangerous — fire claimed theatre after theatre — and within a decade electricity would replace it. But for a while, every play was performed by gaslight, and every scene had its own soft, flickering weather.

— A. Pennifold, Manager
